Dual-system architectures, from psychology to decision models — 2026-10-06

How the idea of a fast System 1 and a slow System 2 moved from psychology into AI, language models, and robots, and where System One decision models such as Jev fit.

October 6, 2026

Two kinds of thinking

In the 1970s, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman catalogued the shortcuts people use when they judge under uncertainty. The shortcuts are fast and usually good enough, but they fail in predictable ways. Psychologists explained the pattern with two kinds of process. One is fast, automatic, and effortless, and handles most of daily life. The other is slow, deliberate, and effortful, uses working memory, and is called on when the fast process is not enough.

Dual-process accounts go back at least to Jonathan Evans's work in the 1980s and Steven Sloman's 1996 paper on two systems of reasoning. Keith Stanovich and Richard West gave the two processes the names System 1 and System 2 in 2000. Kahneman used the names in his 2003 Nobel lecture, "Maps of Bounded Rationality", and made them widely known with Thinking, Fast and Slow in 2011. The names are a simplification. Evans and Stanovich (2013) prefer to speak of two types of process rather than two separate systems, and Kahneman called his two systems useful fictions.

The theory does not claim that the slow process is better. Deliberation is expensive, and a mind that deliberated about everything would act too slowly. Most behavior is fast, and the slow process is used selectively. With practice, a skill that first needs careful thought, such as driving, becomes automatic.

Neuroscience found a similar split. Daw, Niv, and Dayan (2005) described a model-free controller, which acts from cached values (habitual behavior), and a model-based controller, which plans by simulating consequences (goal-directed behavior). Control goes to whichever controller is more certain in the situation at hand. This gave the idea a computational form.

The idea enters AI

In 2017, Anthony, Tian, and Barber published "Thinking Fast and Slow with Deep Learning and Tree Search". A slow tree search plans moves in the game Hex, and a fast neural network learns to imitate those plans. The improved network then guides the next round of search. The same loop appeared in AlphaGo Zero that year.

In his NeurIPS 2019 keynote, "From System 1 Deep Learning to System 2 Deep Learning", Yoshua Bengio argued that deep learning had mastered System 1 tasks such as perception, and that reasoning, planning, and systematic generalization remained open. Booch et al. (AAAI 2021) built the split as an architecture, SOFAI. Fast solvers react from past experience, slow solvers reason and search, and a metacognitive module chooses between them for each problem. Yann LeCun's 2022 position paper uses the same distinction. His Mode-1 produces an action directly from perception, and his Mode-2 plans through a world model.

Language models

Large language models gave the distinction an everyday form. Answering directly is commonly called System 1. Generating reasoning before the answer, as in chain-of-thought prompting, is called System 2. Both run in one network, so the mapping is a convenience rather than a claim about architecture.

The cost of System 2 here is test-time compute, the tokens and time a model spends on each input. Snell et al. (2024) showed that spending more test-time compute can match a model fourteen times larger, at equal total computation, on problems the smaller model can partly solve. Reasoning models followed. Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025) and Qwen3 (April 2025) put both modes in one model, with a switch to turn reasoning on or off and a budget for how many reasoning tokens to spend.

Agent research built the split into separate components. SwiftSage (NeurIPS 2023) pairs a small fine-tuned model that acts quickly with GPT-4, which plans when the small model struggles. DeepMind's Talker-Reasoner (2024) gives conversation to a fast Talker, and multi-step planning and tool use to a slower Reasoner. Meta's "Distilling System 2 into System 1" (2024) fine-tuned a model on the answers its own reasoning produced, so that it could skip the reasoning. This worked for several techniques but not for chain-of-thought reasoning on math.

Robots

Robots need fast control, so the split there runs on two timescales. Figure's Helix runs a 7-billion-parameter vision-language model as System 2 at 7 to 9 times per second, and an 80-million-parameter System 1 that controls the joints 200 times per second. System 2 passes its intent as a latent vector rather than as words or coordinates. NVIDIA's GR00T N1 follows the same design, and Libra-VLA (2026) runs the two systems asynchronously. Hi Robot (ICML 2025) takes a different route, in which the slow model sends plain-language commands to the fast policy.

Four ways System 2 reaches System 1

Across these designs, System 2's knowledge reaches System 1 in four ways.

  • It takes over hard inputs at run time. SOFAI and SwiftSage do this, and in model serving it is called a cascade. Every escalated input pays System 2's cost, and the scheme depends on knowing when System 1 is wrong.
  • It trains System 1 offline. This is distillation, as in expert iteration and Meta's work. It needs training data and retraining whenever the task changes.
  • It sends a signal while both run. Helix and GR00T N1 use a latent vector, and Hi Robot uses language commands. The latent vector needs the two systems trained together.
  • It writes a plan or program that a cheaper executor runs. In ReWOO (2023) and LLMCompiler (ICML 2024), a planner writes the full plan up front and workers carry it out. In Code as Policies (ICRA 2023), a language model writes code that controls a robot. Voyager (NeurIPS 2023) builds a library of skills that it reuses later. In classification by description (Menon and Vondrick, ICLR 2023), a language model writes descriptions of each class that a fast vision model scores. Most of these plans are written for each request, while skill libraries and class descriptions are reused across inputs.

Underneath all four is the same economics. System 2 is costly, so System 1 should handle as much as it reliably can, and System 2's knowledge should reach it at the lowest cost that still works.

Decision models

In September 2026, TypeSafe AI released Jev and called it a System One model. Jev reads its input once and returns a probability for each option the developer listed. It generates no text and does no free-form reasoning. Language models are cast as System Two, handling planning and open-ended writing.

Today the two are joined in three of the four ways above.

  • Design time. A person, not System 2, writes the decision model's questions and the code that acts on the answers. This takes expert effort for every kind of decision.
  • Run time. A low-confidence decision escalates to a language model or a person, which is a cascade.
  • Training. Several open decision models are distilled from the answers of much larger models.

Preprints from the first weeks after release report where this arrangement strains. Yang et al. (2026) found that Jev fails when the right option depends on a latent variable, meaning a fact the input does not state. Jev states that fact correctly when asked for it separately, but does not use it inside a single call. Li et al. (2026) found that cascade routing by confidence works when System 1 is uncertain but fails when it is confidently wrong. Rao and Callison-Burch (2026) found that on Jev's confident mistakes, about 96 percent of language-model judge verdicts repeat the same wrong answer.

For a System 1 that does no free-form reasoning, each existing way therefore has a cost. A person's design takes expert effort for every kind of decision. A cascade pays System 2's cost on every hard input and misses confident errors. Distillation needs data and retraining. The fourth way, a plan written by System 2 and run by a cheaper executor, has mostly been used with executors that reason or run code, not with a decision model.

The open question is whether System 2 can write, once per task, something a decision model can follow on every input with no free-form reasoning, no training, and no person.
