Research postmortem

The binding problem in J-space

Shared activation directions did not establish reliable transfer of which attribute belonged to which entity.

November 2, 2026

The failure: preserving who has what

We tried to pass private information between frozen decision models through sparse J-space messages. In the motivating MiniGrid task, the sender knew a door’s color and the receiver needed to choose the matching key. We did not establish reliable transfer of the relation between an entity and its attribute.

The essential test is the difference between these two scenes:

A: a red door and a blue chair.
B: a blue door and a red chair.

Both contain “door,” “chair,” “red” and “blue.” Only the pairings change. A useful message must preserve which color belongs to which object. This is the binding problem, studied in language-model entity representations.

Why a list of features is insufficient

Suppose an encoder stores only independent object and color features. It would represent both scenes as the same sum: 

door + chair + red + blue

The constituents survive, but their pairings do not. No decoder can recover which scene produced an identical code. Increasing the number of dimensions would not repair an encoding rule that still discards the associations.

This is an illustration, not a measured collision of our actual J-codes. The experiments demonstrated unreliable binding transfer; they did not prove that J-space cannot represent relations or that its sparse codes are inherently too small.

What our interface assumed

Our fixed J-space dictionary represented model hidden states using vocabulary-associated directions derived from a Jacobian lens. The GP32 encoder selected at most 32 nonnegative coefficients per state.

We encoded the receiver’s question states, used their reconstructions to attend to sender states, encoded the weighted sender states as replies, and wrote those replies into the receiver’s existing hidden states. Neither backbone was retrained for this interface.

This assumed that selecting, mixing and reconstructing shared directions would carry the pairings through the entire operation. But an activation reconstruction objective does not explicitly require preserving “red belongs to the door.” Nor does a similarity score necessarily retrieve the attribute bound to the queried entity.

What the tests showed

We permuted four colors across four objects: 24 assignments with the same words but different bindings. Asking for each object’s color produced 96 four-choice development cases.

Messages changed all 96 output distributions, but only one selected answer in the original setup. Attention sometimes found the object word without reliably retrieving its associated color. Correcting weak query and write scales did not establish binding-sensitive decisions.

The positive result, and its limit

A separate test copied all 23 dense fact-token states after sender block 1 into 23 placeholder positions after receiver block 1. With a pretrained, LatentQA-trained oracle decoder, it answered both door-color and red-object questions correctly across all 24 assignments: 48/48 answers. We performed no additional training.

This shows that the full sequence could support binding-sensitive readout. It did not use J-codes, compress the sequence, or demonstrate native action control. It therefore cannot rescue the sparse communication claim.

Why we stopped

The practical failure was the absence of a dependable path from a sender’s entity–attribute pairing to a receiver’s correct decision. We did not isolate whether the decisive problem was encoding, retrieval or writing; lens/model compatibility was also a limitation. A failed interface is not proof that every relation was absent from its representation.

We stopped because further layer and gain variations had not established the required mechanism. A communication code must preserve who has what; detecting that the entities and attributes are present is not enough.
