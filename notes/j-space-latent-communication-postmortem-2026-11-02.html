Research postmortem

The binding problem in J-space

Why we stopped a project on sparse latent communication between frozen decision models, and what its failures actually establish.

November 2, 2026

What we wanted

We wanted one model to pass useful private information to another without sending a text message or retraining their backbones. The sender, TX, would turn its observation into a compact numerical message. The receiver, RX, would combine that message with its own observation and question, then return its normal decision scores.

MiniGrid provided the motivating task: TX knows a target door’s color; RX sees the keys and their locations but lacks that color. A useful message must help RX choose the matching key. Plain text can state the fact directly. The research question was whether an internal representation could provide a reusable communication interface. We did not establish a cost or accuracy advantage over text.

The project ended without a reliable J-space communication interface. It produced some narrow positive results, but none met the original requirement of compact, dependable relational information transfer into a frozen decision model.

The proposed interface

A hidden state is the numerical vector at one token position after a transformer block. In this project, J-space meant representing such states using vocabulary-associated directions derived from a fixed Jacobian lens. Our reference nonnegative gradient-pursuit encoder, GP32, selected at most 32 active coefficients per state. The dictionary and encoder stayed fixed. The motivation came from work on verbalizable representations and global workspaces; the released lenses supplied concrete artifacts to investigate.

The final J-space attention design had three operations:

  1. Encode RX’s question-position states as sparse J-codes, then reconstruct vectors to use as queries.
  2. Compare each query with TX’s token states, apply softmax to the scores, and encode the resulting weighted TX state as a sparse reply.
  3. Write the reply into the corresponding existing RX positions through the fixed dictionary, preserving the incoming reconstruction remainder and enforcing the declared sparse, nonnegative endpoint.

This was an attention-like computation built outside the model’s native attention. It had no learned query/key alignment. Sharing directions was supposed to make the extracted message readable and writable across contexts. That was the assumption the experiments failed to establish.

The distinction that mattered: presence versus binding

Consider two scenes:

Scene A: a red door and a blue chair.
Scene B: a blue door and a red chair.

Both contain exactly the same objects and colors. The difference is which color belongs to which object. This association is the binding. Finding features for “door,” “chair,” “red” and “blue” does not, by itself, recover it.

An encoding that simply sums independent object and color features would give both scenes the same vector: 

door + chair + red + blue

Once the two scenes have the same code, no decoder can tell them apart from that code alone. A larger space would not fix an encoder that still throws away the pairings. Research on entity binding in language models studies mechanisms that preserve these associations rather than just their constituent features.

This sum is an illustration, not a measured collision of our J-codes. Our experiments showed unreliable binding readout and transfer. They did not prove that the sparse representation erased every relation, or that J-space is inherently too small to hold one.

What failed in the J-space experiments

We reduced the problem to four object–color facts. All 24 permutations of four colors across four objects gave the same word inventory while changing the bindings. Asking for each object’s color produced 96 forced-choice cases. These were development tests, not an independent confirmation set.

The original messages changed all 96 output distributions, but changed only one selected answer. Changing an activation was therefore not evidence of useful communication. The best calibration stayed close to the 25% reference level of the four-choice test.

Three problems were visible in the saved traces:

  • Weak queries and writes. The decoded query norms were about 0.1% of the native state norms, and the initial softmax weights were almost uniform. Correcting routing and write scales did not solve the task. These norm ratios are not percentages of semantic information.
  • Retrieval without dependable binding. Calibrated attention sometimes found the queried object word, but did not reliably find its associated color. A causal object-token state also cannot contain a color that occurs later in its input.
  • Writing without demonstrated interpretation. A valid sparse endpoint and a preserved reconstruction remainder qualified the numerical operation. They did not establish that RX treated the edit as the intended fact or used it to choose the right action.

Earlier trained reading experiments also found partial rather than general recovery. In one inspected, equal-color-count exchange subset, a selected sparse reader answered both sides correctly in only 21 of 192 pairs. Specialized readers did succeed under supplied mention positions and programmed two-object rules. Those narrower successes do not support the stronger claim that relations are absent from J-space; they also did not establish an autonomous communication interface.

The positive result we should keep

We then tested a separate dense readout path using a released Gemma 4 31B instruction-model Activation Oracle, trained partly on LatentQA. This is a previously trained decoder, not the original LatentQA Llama checkpoint and not an untrained native decision model. We performed no additional training in these final tests. The release’s implementation report describes its trained interface.

The source contained only these four sentences, with their colors permuted:

The door is red.
The chair is blue.
The table is green.
The box is yellow.

They occupy 23 fact-token positions in the tested tokenizer. RX received a layer label, 23 ordinary ? placeholder tokens and one question: “What color is the door?” or “What object is described as red?” It never received the source facts as text. Each of the same 24 assignments generated two answers, giving 48 answers per configuration.

Block numbers are zero-based. In replacement, we copied each TX fact-position state after the stated block directly into its corresponding RX placeholder position after the matching block, without normalization or addition. The 1 → 1 result correctly tracked all tested color swaps. It is a genuine positive result for reading the full dense sequence with this trained oracle.

It is not compact communication: 23 vectors of width 5,376 in BF16 require 247,296 tensor bytes, before metadata. It also uses extra placeholder positions, no J-codes, and generated answers rather than native action scores. Reading all facts through this route does not establish a one-vector encoder, a J-space channel, or control of a complete task.

The attention-only tests used a different operation: RX’s native queries attended jointly to its own keys and values and a separate bank containing TX’s 23 fact-token keys and values. Only one RX block could access that bank; no hidden states were replaced. The tested blocks were sliding-attention blocks, and TX’s original rotary positions were retained. The prompt’s Layer 45 framing stayed fixed between these two attention runs; the successful replacement-at-1 run used Layer 1 framing. This is not a perfectly isolated replacement-versus-attention comparison, nor a test of every possible cross-attention design.

What the failures do not prove

We did not isolate a single fundamental cause. The initial lens was fitted on the base model, while the communication experiments used an instruction checkpoint. A later instruction-model lens did not pin its fitting-model revision. Its short-prompt “red” position also fell inside the first 16 positions excluded by the stated fitting recipe. A missing “door” entry in a sparse readout was therefore not proof that the underlying state lacked the relation.

Implementation checks mattered too. We corrected an offline logit-lens softcap precision mismatch without changing prior GP32 or native decision results. An initial attention override failed the native-mask guard before scoring; the repaired path preserved native masks and passed exact no-message hidden-state parity. Numerical checks made the reported tests interpretable, but did not make their answers correct.

The final panels were small, fixed-template development evidence. They establish neither broad generalization nor a theorem about representational capacity. We have no measured code collision proving that two different bindings became identical, and a failed decoder alone cannot prove the information is absent.

Why we stopped

We could keep changing layers and gains, but the experiments had not supplied a dependable mechanism for extracting, preserving and writing a relation. The project’s practical requirement was reliable decision support, not a changed probability distribution or an occasional correct answer.

The failure was in the tested communication protocol. J-space provided activation directions. It did not establish that selecting and mixing those directions preserved “red belongs to the door,” or that writing the resulting mixture made RX compute the answer it would obtain from the original facts.

We stopped this branch and retained the failed tests, implementation corrections and narrow positive results. The useful conclusion is a requirement for a future interface: demonstrate that messages distinguish matched binding swaps and cause the receiver to use those distinctions, before claiming a general channel for latent communication.
